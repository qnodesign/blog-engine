The last autumn weekend in the mountains is over. You close the shutters, turn the heating down, lock the door and drive home. The next visit is planned for the ski holidays in February.

In between, there are weeks when nobody is there. And somewhere in January, a cold night comes that is a few degrees colder than the house was built for.

## The damage you don't see

When water freezes in a pipe, it expands, and the pipe can burst. The tricky part, as Swiss insurers point out: you often don't notice when it happens. The pipe is plugged with ice. Only when it thaws does the water start running, sometimes for days, through an empty flat, into the walls and into the flat below.

## Why "frost protection" on the radiator isn't enough

Many people turn the radiator valve to the snowflake symbol and feel safe. AXA explains why that's not enough: the frost setting protects the radiator, not the pipes. Their advice is never to switch the heating off completely and to keep it at least on level 1.

The real risk sits where the thermostat isn't. A pipe in an outside wall. The bathroom on the north side. The utility room, the garage, the spot where the water main comes into the house. The living room can be at a comfortable 10 degrees while the pipe behind the bathroom wall is at zero.

## Frost protection with a few sensors

You can't be in the mountains and in Zurich at the same time. But your home can tell you how it's doing:

- **Temperature sensors in the coldest spots,** not the living room. If the bathroom drops below 5 degrees, you get a message while there's still time to act.
- **Leak sensors** under the boiler, the washing machine and the kitchen sink. If water appears, you know within seconds, not at the next visit.
- **Heating you can check from home.** See the temperature in every room, and raise it by a few degrees before a cold snap.
- **A warm house when you arrive.** The heating starts in good time before you come, as described in [my post on energy](/en/insights/energy-efficiency/).
- **A short weekly note: everything's fine.** Often that's the most valuable message of all.

If you want to go one step further, a plumber can fit a motorised main valve that closes the water automatically when a leak sensor raises the alarm. That's building work, so it's one for owners, not tenants.

## Who goes there when the alert comes?

A message on your phone helps only if someone can act on it. So before winter, it's worth agreeing on one thing: who has a key and can go and look? A neighbour, the caretaker, someone from the village.

## A word on insurance

Frost damage isn't covered by the cantonal building insurance, because frost isn't treated as a natural hazard. It falls under a separate building water insurance, plus your household contents insurance for furniture and belongings. And insurers expect you to take reasonable precautions. AXA even mentions electronic monitoring of heating and valves for holiday homes. What exactly your policy requires is worth a call to your insurer. I'm not an insurance adviser.

## Start before the first cold snap

You don't need a big system for this. Two or three temperature sensors, two leak sensors and the small hub that ties them together.
